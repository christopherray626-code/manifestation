# How the app writes affirmations

The Claude writer (`api/write.ts`) follows this guide. Chris will add what he knows; put it under
"From Chris" and fold it into the prompt.

## What the research says

- **Mix "I" with "you".** Talking to yourself in the second person ("You can do this") beat the first
  person for performance and intentions in Dolcos et al. (2014). The researchers think "you" calls up
  memories of being encouraged by others. "I" lines own the identity; "you" lines feel like support.
  So: mostly "I", with about a third in "you". Use the person's name now and then, never every line.
- **Keep it believable.** In Wood, Perunovic & Lee (2009), repeating "I'm a lovable person" made people
  with low self-esteem feel *worse*: a claim far from what you believe gets argued with. Mix fully
  present lines with "bridge" lines that move toward it: "I am becoming…", "more and more",
  "every day it gets easier to…", "I am learning to…".
- **Put the goal in the assumption, not the claim.** The mind argues with what a sentence asserts, not
  with what it takes for granted (presupposition). "When people notice how calm I am, they relax too"
  never claims "I am calm"; it assumes it. Words that carry an assumption: *already, still, again,
  now that, when, as, notice, realize, remember, how easily, even more*.
- **Give a reason.** In Langer's copy-machine study, a request with "because" got ~94% yes against ~60%
  without, even when the reason was empty. A line with a reason sticks: "I attract money because I
  create real value."
- **Rhythm reads as truth.** Rhyming sayings were rated more true than the same idea unrhymed
  (McGlone & Tofighbakhsh, 2000): things that are easy to process feel true. Short, even, spoken
  rhythm; repetition of a sound or a structure ("I am ready. I am able. I am here."); the odd rhyme.
- **Say what you want, not what you don't.** "I am calm", not "I am not anxious": the key word lands.
- **Be concrete and sensory.** Small real moments ("the keys in my hand", "the notification that the
  payment cleared") are easier to picture, and so to feel, than abstractions.
- **Identity over outcome.** "I am the kind of person who…" frames the goal as who you are, which
  carries the habits with it.

## Persuasion patterns used (all aimed at the listener's own mind)

- **Presupposition:** "Now that money comes easily, I get to choose what matters."
- **Embedded command:** a command inside a softer sentence, e.g. "You can *feel confident* right now."
- **Awareness words:** "You're starting to notice how naturally it comes."
- **Double bind (both choices lead to the goal):** "Whether it arrives this week or the next, it's
  already on its way."
- **Future pacing:** "Tomorrow you'll wake up and it will still be true."
- **Truism then lead:** something plainly true, then the step: "You've made it through every hard
  day so far, and you're getting stronger."
- **Tag:** "You already know this, don't you."

## From Chris

Chris's own style (from examples he shared; never copy his lines, write new ones the same way):

- **Spoken to "you"**, like a friend who can't believe how good your life is.
- **"Why" questions that assume it's already true** ("Why does it come to you so easily?"), known as
  afformations. The mind goes looking for the answer, and the question takes the goal for granted.
- **"You've always been..."**: the goal as settled history, not something new.
- **Abundance so big it's almost a problem**: you can't keep up with it, you have to figure out what to
  do with it all.
- **Closure**: "It's done." Said once, with weight.
- **Identity**: "it's who you are", "you were born for this".
- **Gratitude and feeling**: "it feels amazing", "you're so grateful".
- **A dash for an afterthought that adds emotion**: "..., it's like magic."

The tracks for money, wealth, success, confidence, love, luck and glow up are written this way.
